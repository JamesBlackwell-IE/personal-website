Research

My background is in the mechanics of soft tissues: how they deform and respond to forces. My current research applies that perspective to clinical ultrasound, studying what measurements of tissue stiffness and blood flow can tell us about disease.

Liver elastography

I work with clinical colleagues to investigate ultrasound measurements of liver stiffness in children after liver transplantation and in patients with Fontan circulation. Our published work includes a paediatric transplant study, a review of elastography in Fontan-associated liver disease, and a case report illustrating the challenges of interpreting these measurements.

Ongoing researchA central question is how to distinguish changes in liver tissue from the effects of altered circulation. In Fontan patients, congestion can also increase liver stiffness. We are investigating how elastography can be interpreted alongside blood-flow measurements, other ultrasound findings and clinical information.

Ophthalmic artery Doppler

Doppler ultrasound provides information about blood flow in the ophthalmic artery, which supplies the eye. Our published systematic review examined first-trimester measurements for predicting early-onset and preterm preeclampsia, assessing the evidence and limitations of this approach.

Ongoing researchWe are also investigating the relationship between ophthalmic artery Doppler measurements and changes in the small retinal blood vessels in diabetes. This work combines ultrasound with optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), which images the retinal microvasculature.

Related publication

  • First-trimester ophthalmic artery Doppler for predicting preeclampsia ↗BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth · 2026 · Systematic review and critical appraisal

Soft tissue mechanics

My earlier research at the University of Galway explored how soft materials deform under load, using experiments and mathematical modelling. This included work on the Poynting effect: the tendency of a material to change length when twisted, and how geometry can alter that response.

That background informs my current interest in ultrasound elastography. Connecting the mechanics of tissue with the measurements we obtain is an important part of understanding what those measurements mean.
